1

Medial Meniscal Horizontal Tear (Posterior Horn)

Primary Diagnosis

MRI Finding: Horizontal tear extending to tibial surface with menisco-capsular oedema. No displaced flap.

Healthy Normal Meniscus Intact Wedge

Smooth, rubbery C-shaped fibrocartilage wedge with solid unbroken tissue fibres absorbing 100% of downward impact.

Rekha's Finding: Horizontal Tear Stable, Non-Displaced

A horizontal cleavage line splits upper and lower layers. Key relief: Cushion is completely in place (no displaced flap or locking fragment).

What does this mean in plain English?

The medial meniscus is a shock-absorbing C-shaped fibrocartilage cushion sitting on the inner side of your knee. A horizontal tear splits the cushion horizontally (like cutting a sandwich bagel in half horizontally).

Crucial positive note: The MRI confirms there is no detached flap and no meniscal extrusion (it has not slipped out of place). It stays where it belongs, meaning conservative rehabilitation with muscle-strengthening has a high chance of success!

Care Advice & Exercises
  • ✔ Do: Straight leg raises, quad sets, and hamstring bridges to take pressure off the meniscus.
  • ✖ Avoid for now: Deep twisting, sudden pivots on a planted foot, or deep squats beyond 90 degrees.

2

Grade 4 Parapatellar Chondrolysis (Kneecap Cartilage)

Cartilage Wear

MRI Finding: Full-thickness loss of cartilage alongside the kneecap. Note: Main tibiofemoral cartilage is intact!

Healthy Patellar Cartilage Full 3-4mm Cushion

Cartilage is thick and glassy-smooth, allowing the kneecap to glide effortlessly over the femur groove with zero resistance.

Rekha's Finding: Grade 4 Wear Side Edge Wear

Cartilage on the side edge (parapatellar) has worn thin. Quad training (VMO) re-centers the kneecap so it glides away from this area.

What does this mean in plain English?

Cartilage is the smooth coating that helps bones glide smoothly against each other. "Grade 4" means in one specific zone alongside the kneecap (parapatellar groove), the cartilage layer has worn thin down to the underlying bone.

The great news is that the main joint cartilage (tibiofemoral) between your upper leg bone and shin bone is fully preserved! Strengthening the vastus medialis (inner quad) and glutes improves kneecap tracking so it glides smoothly without rubbing.

Care Advice & Exercises
  • ✔ Do: Low-impact quad sets, gentle isometric wall sits (shallow), and glute bridges.
  • ✖ Avoid for now: Heavy lunges, high-impact running, or stairs when knees are feeling inflamed.

3

Hoffa’s Fat Pad Mild Oedema

Irritation / Pinch

MRI Finding: Mild superolateral oedema (swelling) within Hoffa's infrapatellar fat pad.

Calm Normal Fat Pad

Natural soft fatty pillow behind patellar tendon with no pinching or nerve irritation.

Rekha's Finding: Mild Oedema

Fluid infiltration makes it plump, creating a pinching sensation when straightening knee fully.

What does this mean in plain English?

Hoffa’s fat pad is a rich, soft cushion packed with nerve endings located right behind and below the kneecap tendon. When inflamed ("oedema"), it can be sensitive when extending the knee fully or kneeling.

Because this fat pad is sensitive, aggressive hyperextension (locking the knee backward) should be avoided while it calms down. Cold therapy and gentle range-of-motion work quickly alleviate this swelling.

Care Advice & Exercises
  • ✔ Do: Ice packs (15 mins after activity), gentle heel slides, avoiding hyperextension lock.
  • ✖ Avoid: Prolonged kneeling on hard surfaces without padding, snapping the knee back forcefully.

4

Prepatellar Bursitis (Kneecap Cushion Swelling)

Front Knee Fluid

MRI Finding: Fluid and inflammation in the bursa sac located directly on the front surface of the kneecap.

Normal Flat Bursa

Almost paper-thin, containing just enough natural lubricant to prevent skin chafing.

Rekha's Finding: Inflamed Bursitis

Fluid buildup creates a visible/palpable soft bulge in front of kneecap. Fully settles with rest and ice.

What does this mean in plain English?

A bursa is a thin, fluid-filled pouch designed to reduce friction between the skin and the kneecap bone. When irritated (from pressure, repetitive bending, or friction), it fills with extra fluid, creating puffiness in front of the kneecap.

Bursitis is very treatable conservatively through rest, ice, compression, and keeping excessive direct pressure off the front of the knee.

Care Advice & Exercises
  • ✔ Do: Elevation, cold compress wrap for 15-20 mins, gentle non-weight bearing motion.
  • ✖ Avoid: Direct kneeling on floor/carpet; wear a soft knee pad if ever needing to kneel.
5

MCL (Medial Collateral Ligament) Low-Grade Chronic Sprain

Mild Ligament Strain

MRI Finding: Low-grade, chronic sprain. Ligament fibres remain intact with internal joint stability preserved.

What does this mean in plain English?

The MCL runs down the inside of your knee, preventing the knee from collapsing inward. "Chronic low-grade" means it has experienced mild overstretching in the past, but is not torn through and maintains your knee's internal stability.

Rebuilding hip abductor strength (side glutes) prevents the knee from caving inwards (valgus collapse), giving your MCL complete protection.

Care Advice & Exercises
  • ✔ Do: Clamshells and side leg raises to fortify gluteus medius for inner knee alignment.
  • ✖ Avoid: Wide stance squats where knees collapse inward toward each other.

Small Joint Effusion & Posteromedial Swelling

Reactive Fluid

MRI Finding: Small intra-articular fluid buildup. Reassuring: No Baker’s cyst detected.

What does this mean in plain English?

When any structure inside the joint is irritated, the knee's synovial lining generates extra fluid — like a natural internal water-cushion. It is described as "small", which is mild and expected.

Notice also that there is no Baker's cyst (no fluid bulge protruding into the back hollow of your knee). As the surrounding tissues strengthen, the joint absorbs this fluid naturally.

Care Advice & Exercises
  • ✔ Do: Gentle ankle pumps and calf raises to stimulate venous pump and lymphatic drainage.
  • ✖ Avoid: Long periods of standing completely motionless.
Rehabilitation Plan

Targeted Daily Exercise Program

Safe, physiotherapist-endorsed exercises selected specifically for your meniscus, cartilage, and fat pad recovery.

Low-Impact & Joint-Friendly

Rekha's Golden Rules for Exercise:

1. Pain-Free Range: A mild muscle burn is normal; sharp pain or pinch is your signal to back off.
2. Smooth & Controlled: Never bounce or snap your knee locked. Focus on slow 3-second counts.
3. Ice Afterwards: Apply a cold pack wrapped in a towel for 10-15 minutes if warmth or puffiness occurs.
Essential Phase 1 Target: Quad & Kneecap Glide

1. Isometric Quad Sets (Towel Press)

Helps reactivate your front thigh muscles without moving the joint, protecting both the meniscus and patellar cartilage from friction.

How to do it:

  1. Sit on your bed or floor with your right leg stretched out flat.
  2. Place a small rolled towel directly under your knee.
  3. Tighten your thigh muscle by pressing the back of your knee gently into the towel.
  4. Hold firmly for 5 seconds, then slowly release.
Frequency: 2 sets of 10-12 repetitions, twice daily.
Watch Video: Quad Sets Technique
Meniscus Safe Target: Hip & Thigh Strength

2. Straight Leg Raises (SLR)

Strengthens the entire anterior chain and hip flexor without bending the knee joint, preventing friction against the posterior horn meniscus tear.

How to do it:

  1. Lie flat on your back. Bend your left (good) knee with foot on the floor.
  2. Keep your right knee straight and toes pointed up.
  3. Lock your quad and smoothly lift the right leg 8 to 12 inches off the ground.
  4. Hold for 2-3 seconds at the top, then lower slowly.
Frequency: 2 sets of 10 reps per session.
Watch Video: Straight Leg Raise Form
Mobility & Fluid Flow Target: Range of Motion

3. Gentle Towel / Sock Heel Slides

Helps pump stagnant fluid (effusion & fat pad irritation) out of the knee joint while gently restoring bending flexibility.

How to do it:

  1. Sit or lie on a smooth surface wearing socks (or use a small towel under heel).
  2. Slowly slide your right heel toward your buttock, bending the knee within comfortable limits.
  3. Pause for 3-5 seconds where comfortable, feeling a mild stretch without pain.
  4. Slowly slide back out straight.
Frequency: 10 to 12 slow repetitions, 1-2 times daily.
Watch Video: Gentle Heel Slides Guide
MCL & Patella Support Target: Gluteus Medius

4. Side-Lying Clamshells

Strengthens hip abductors. This prevents your knee from collapsing inward during walking, taking the tension off your sprained MCL and patellar groove.

How to do it:

  1. Lie on your left (uninjured) side with both knees bent at a 45-degree angle.
  2. Keep your feet glued together.
  3. Slowly lift your top (right) knee toward the ceiling while keeping pelvis steady (don’t roll backwards).
  4. Hold for 2 seconds, then lower smoothly.
Frequency: 2 sets of 12 reps on each side.
Watch Video: Perfect Clamshell Form
Posterior Stability Target: Glutes & Hamstrings

5. Bilateral Glute Bridges

Strengthens posterior chain without placing direct shearing forces across the knee joint or patella.

How to do it:

  1. Lie on your back with both knees bent, feet flat hip-width apart.
  2. Engage your core and squeeze your glutes.
  3. Drive through your heels to lift your hips until your thighs and torso align.
  4. Hold for 3 seconds, then slowly lower down.
Frequency: 2 sets of 10 reps.
Watch Video: Glute Bridge for Knee Pain
VMO Awakening Target: Vastus Medialis (VMO)

6. Seated Terminal Knee Extension

Helps strengthen the inner quadriceps muscle that controls kneecap positioning, keeping it centered away from the worn cartilage zone.

How to do it:

  1. Sit upright in a supportive chair with knees bent at 90 degrees.
  2. Slowly straighten your right leg out in front until it is almost horizontal.
  3. Focus on squeezing the muscle just above the inside of your kneecap for 3 seconds.
  4. Lower gently back to floor.
Frequency: 2 sets of 10 reps.
